Chapter 1Introduction

1.1 Background of the Study

Sudden cardiac arrest is one of the leading causes of preventable death worldwide. The chance of survival depends heavily on how quickly a bystander begins cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), because every minute without effective chest compressions sharply reduces the likelihood of recovery. The American Heart Association (AHA) Guidelines for CPR and Emergency Cardiovascular Care, updated in October 2025, recommend that an adult rescuer check the scene and the person, call for help and an automated external defibrillator (AED), and then push hard and fast: at least 5 cm (2 inches) deep, at 100 to 120 compressions per minute, allowing full chest recoil and keeping interruptions to a minimum [Cite: AHA, 2025]. Effective compressions are delivered with straight, locked elbows so that the rescuer's body weight is transferred directly through the arms to the patient's chest.

Knowing these standards is not enough. CPR is a psychomotor skill, so it must be practiced repeatedly with feedback until correct posture and rhythm become muscle memory. Commercial CPR training manikins with sensors and feedback devices are effective for this purpose, but they are expensive. In the Philippines, such equipment can cost tens of thousands of pesos per unit, which places it beyond the reach of many public schools, student safety clubs and barangay emergency responders. As a result, many learners receive only lectures or short demonstrations and rarely get the individual, corrective practice that makes CPR training effective.

Advances in computer vision offer a low-cost alternative. Lightweight pose-estimation models such as Google's MediaPipe Pose Landmarker can detect 33 body landmarks in real time from an ordinary webcam [Cite: Bazarevsky et al., 2020; Lugaresi et al., 2019]. Through the MediaPipe Tasks Vision library, the model now runs directly inside a web browser using WebAssembly and the computer's graphics processor, without a cloud connection or any installation beyond the browser. This makes it possible to measure posture (elbow angle) and movement rhythm (compression cadence) on hardware that most schools already own.

Project L.I.F.E. (Localized Intelligent First-aid Evaluator) applies this idea. It is an offline system in which a standard laptop webcam and in-browser pose estimation evaluate a trainee practicing compressions on a low-cost DIY chest prop. The system gives real-time visual and optional spoken coaching, tracks the trainee's hands and fingers, guides each session step by step through the 2025 AHA adult CPR sequence, and records results in a local database through a web dashboard built with React and TypeScript and a Node.js server using SQLite. Trainers can monitor progress, rank performance and export data, and trainees can sign in to review their own progress and practise on their own, all without internet access or expensive equipment; no camera video ever leaves the computer.

1.2 Statement of the Problem

The study aims to address the lack of affordable, feedback-capable CPR training tools for resource-constrained communities. Specifically, it seeks to answer the following questions:

  1. What are the limitations of current CPR training methods and tools available to public schools and community responders in terms of cost, accessibility and feedback?
  2. How can a webcam-based, in-browser pose-estimation system be designed to detect elbow extension and compression cadence during CPR practice and to coach the trainee in real time?
  3. How accurate is the developed system in detecting locked elbows and compression rate compared with a reference method (a trained observer and a metronome-timed count)?
  4. How can session results, trainee records, procedure adherence and role-based access for administrators, trainers and trainees be stored, secured and displayed through a local, offline web dashboard?
  5. How do trainers and trainees evaluate the system in terms of functional suitability, performance efficiency, usability, reliability, security and portability?

1.3 Objectives of the Study

General Objective. To design, develop and evaluate Project L.I.F.E., an offline computer-vision CPR training evaluator that provides real-time feedback on compression posture and cadence using a standard webcam and web browser.

Specific Objectives.

  1. To develop a computer-vision module in TypeScript using MediaPipe Tasks Vision (Pose Landmarker and Hand Landmarker) that runs in the browser, detects upper-body and hand landmarks, and computes elbow angle, compression cadence and hand position in real time.
  2. To implement real-time feedback on the live camera view, including elbow-lock status, current cadence (BPM), a single prioritized coaching cue (for example, "Push faster", "Lock your elbows"), an optional metronome and optional spoken cues, together with step-by-step guidance through the 2025 AHA adult CPR sequence (breath prompts at the correct compression-to-breath ratio, a guided AED pause and the 2-minute compressor switch).
  3. To build a local web dashboard (React and TypeScript, served by Node.js and Express with an SQLite database) that stores trainee profiles and consent, sessions, per-compression data and the trainer-confirmed CPR sequence; provides a live session monitor, leaderboard, history, PDF reports with CSV exports, and a Certificate of Practice Completion; gives administrators, trainers and trainees role-appropriate access, including trainee sign-in accounts for reviewing their own progress and self-practice; and operates entirely offline.
  4. To design a low-cost DIY chest prop that can serve as a substitute for a commercial manikin during practice.
  5. To evaluate the system's detection accuracy (confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1-score and BPM error), trainees' adherence to the CPR procedure, and the system's quality using the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model.

1.4 Significance of the Study

Trainees and students. They receive immediate, objective feedback on posture, rhythm and the order of the CPR steps, which helps them correct mistakes while practicing rather than after a delayed instructor review. With their own sign-in, they can also review their progress and practise between supervised sessions.

Trainers and student safety clubs. They gain a data-logged way to track each trainee's progress over time, rank performance and support decisions on readiness for formal certification.

Public schools. The system lowers the cost barrier by using existing laptops, a free web browser and inexpensive materials, supporting first-aid education in schools with limited budgets and unreliable internet.

Barangay responders and the community. Better-trained responders improve local emergency readiness and may increase the chance of survival for cardiac arrest victims.

Future researchers. The study provides a documented, tested baseline (including open evaluation tooling) for low-cost, vision-based skills training that can be extended to other first-aid procedures.

The study also contributes to the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, particularly SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 4 (Quality Education).

1.5 Scope and Delimitations

Scope.

  • Real-time detection of elbow extension (locked or not locked) and compression cadence (target 100 to 120 BPM) for a single trainee at a time, with a default practice session of 2 minutes, matching the AHA recommendation to switch compressors every 2 minutes.
  • Use of a standard laptop webcam positioned at a side or 45-degree view of the trainee's upper body.
  • Offline operation in a web browser (Chrome or Edge) on one computer, with a local Node.js server and an SQLite database; the system is started with a single desktop shortcut.
  • A step-by-step guide through the 2025 AHA adult CPR sequence during each session: trainer-confirmed scene, responsiveness, breathing and emergency-call steps; camera-tracked compressions; cycle counting with a breath prompt at the ratio for the selected victim and number of rescuers (30:2, or 15:2 for a child or infant with two rescuers), or hands-only CPR; a guided AED pause; and a 2-minute compressor-switch signal.
  • Hand and finger tracking to check that the trainee's hands are stacked, one on top of the other.
  • Practice on a DIY chest prop (high-density foam, stacked cardboard, or a rolled towel inside a shirt) marked with a target X.
  • Optional sign-in accounts for trainees, created by an administrator, through which each trainee views their own progress and runs self-practice sessions.
  • Logging of trainee profiles and consent, session results, per-compression data, leaderboard ranking, PDF reports and CSV exports for analysis, and a Certificate of Practice Completion downloadable as a PDF.

Delimitations.

  • The system is a training aid, not a medical device. It does not certify real-life clinical competence, and it is not used on real patients.
  • Compression depth, chest recoil, the exact position of the hands on the center of the chest and the effectiveness of rescue breaths are not measured, because a single 2D webcam cannot reliably measure them; these remain the trainer's responsibility to observe. The system checks only that the hands are stacked and prompts for breaths in 30:2 mode.
  • The elbow angle is measured in two dimensions and is most accurate from a side view; only the arm nearest the camera is evaluated.
  • Camera evaluation covers adult CPR technique only. Selecting a child or infant victim changes only the compression-to-breath ratio and cycle counting for practice; child and infant compression technique (including infant two-finger or two-thumb compressions) is not evaluated.
  • Accuracy may vary with lighting, camera angle, clothing and occlusion; testing is done under controlled indoor conditions.
  • The study is limited to participants from the selected school or organization in Iligan City, and results may not generalize to all populations.

1.6 Definition of Terms

  • Accuracy score. The percentage of scorable compressions in a session that meet both the posture and cadence criteria.
  • AED (Automated External Defibrillator). A portable device that analyzes the heart rhythm and, if needed, delivers a shock while giving voice prompts.
  • CPR cycle. One set of chest compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths; for an adult, 30 compressions and 2 breaths (ratio 30:2). About 5 cycles of 30:2 take roughly 2 minutes.
  • Compression cadence. The number of chest compressions delivered per minute, expressed in beats per minute (BPM).
  • CPR (Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation). An emergency procedure of chest compressions, with rescue breaths by trained rescuers, that maintains blood flow when the heart stops.
  • DIY chest prop. A low-cost improvised chest simulator used in place of a commercial CPR manikin.
  • Hand Landmarker. A MediaPipe model that estimates 21 landmarks for each detected hand (the wrist and four points per finger); used to check that the hands are stacked.
  • Hands-only CPR. Continuous chest compressions without rescue breaths, recommended for untrained or unwilling rescuers.
  • Leaderboard. A ranked list of trainees by their average accuracy, with an option to hide names when displayed publicly.
  • Locked elbows. An arm position in which the elbows are fully extended, allowing the rescuer's body weight to be transferred through straight arms; in this system, an elbow angle at or above a calibrated threshold (default 160 degrees).
  • MediaPipe Pose Landmarker. An open-source Google model, available through the MediaPipe Tasks Vision library, that estimates 33 body landmarks, each with coordinates and a visibility score, from images or video in real time.
  • Offline system. A system that runs entirely on local hardware without needing an internet connection.
  • Procedure adherence score. The percentage of applicable CPR procedure items (for example, steps done in order, breaths after every cycle, pauses under 10 seconds) that a trainee followed in a session; reported separately from the accuracy score.
  • Pose estimation. A computer-vision technique that locates key body joints in images or video.
  • Scorable compression. A detected compression for which both the elbow angle and the cadence could be measured; compressions with the elbow out of view are reported separately as unscorable.
  • Self-practice session. A session started by a trainee on their own sign-in account. It is saved to the trainee's progress and the session history but excluded from the leaderboard and dashboard statistics.
  • SQLite. A self-contained, file-based relational database engine that needs no separate database server.
  • Supervised session. A session run by a trainer or administrator; only supervised sessions are used for rankings and dashboard statistics.
  • WebAssembly. A portable binary format that lets compiled code, such as the pose model's runtime, execute at near-native speed inside a web browser.

2.1 Related Literature

2.1.1 Cardiac Arrest and the Adult CPR Sequence

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest has a low survival rate, but prompt bystander CPR can substantially improve outcomes. The 2025 AHA guidelines describe a sequence for adult bystanders: (1) make sure the scene is safe, tap the person and shout, and treat absent or gasping breathing as cardiac arrest; (2) call the emergency number (911 in the Philippines) and send someone for an AED; (3) start chest compressions with the heel of one hand on the center of the chest and the other hand on top, pushing at least 5 cm deep at 100 to 120 per minute with full recoil and minimal interruptions; (4) if trained, give cycles of 30 compressions to 2 breaths, otherwise continue hands-only CPR, avoiding excessive breaths; (5) use the AED as soon as it arrives and resume CPR immediately after a shock or a no-shock advisory; and (6) keep going, switching compressors every 2 minutes, until responders take over or the person recovers [Cite: AHA, 2025]. One cycle consists of 30 compressions and 2 breaths. The ratio depends on the victim and the number of rescuers (Table 1): 30:2 for adults with one or two rescuers, and for children and infants with one rescuer; 15:2 for children and infants when two rescuers are present. At 100 to 120 compressions per minute, about 5 cycles of 30:2 take roughly 2 minutes, after which the rescuers check the person and switch compressors, repeating until help arrives or the person shows signs of life. The guidelines stress that compression quality (rate, depth, recoil and minimal interruptions) is a major determinant of survival [Cite: Philippine Heart Association or Philippine Red Cross basic life support materials].

2.1.2 CPR Training and Feedback

Studies in resuscitation education generally find that hands-on practice with real-time feedback produces better skill acquisition and retention than lecture-only or video-only instruction, and that audio prompts such as a metronome help learners hold the recommended rate. However, commercial feedback manikins and devices carry a significant cost [Cite: studies on CPR feedback devices, metronome guidance and deliberate practice].

2.1.3 Computer Vision and Human Pose Estimation

Human pose estimation locates body joints in images or video. Modern lightweight models run in real time on consumer hardware. MediaPipe Pose, based on the BlazePose architecture, returns 33 landmarks, each with normalized coordinates and a visibility score, which makes it suitable for joint-angle computation on ordinary laptops (Bazarevsky et al., 2020). The MediaPipe framework organizes perception models into efficient pipelines (Lugaresi et al., 2019), and its Tasks Vision library runs the Pose Landmarker in the browser through WebAssembly with optional GPU acceleration, removing the need for a Python installation or a server-side model.

2.1.4 Pose Estimation in Exercise and Skill Coaching

Pose-estimation systems have been used for exercise-form correction, physical therapy and sports coaching. These systems typically compute joint angles from landmark triplets and count repetitions from the periodic movement of a landmark. The same approach applies to CPR, where compressions form a repetitive up-and-down motion of the upper body and wrists [Cite: fitness posture-correction and rehabilitation studies].

2.1.5 Offline and Low-Resource Educational Technology

Many schools in developing regions have limited or unstable internet access. Locally hosted web applications, in which a small server and a file-based database run on the same computer as the browser, allow tools to be deployed without cloud infrastructure or separate database servers. This lowers cost, simplifies setup to a single desktop shortcut, and avoids the privacy risk of uploading trainee video [Cite: studies on offline learning tools in low-resource settings].

2.1.6 Frugal Innovation

Frugal innovation focuses on delivering functional solutions at a fraction of conventional cost by using available resources. Replacing a specialized manikin with a DIY prop and free software on an existing laptop reflects this approach [Cite: frugal innovation literature].

2.3 Synthesis and Research Gap

The literature shows that (1) CPR quality depends on correct rate and posture within a defined rescue sequence, (2) feedback-based practice improves learning, (3) pose estimation can measure body posture and movement from ordinary cameras, now even inside a web browser, and (4) cost and connectivity limit access to training tools in many communities. Existing solutions rely either on expensive sensor hardware or on specialized cameras, technical installations and cloud services. There is little work on a fully offline, webcam-only, browser-based evaluator that combines posture and cadence feedback, guidance through the full CPR sequence, and a secure local data-logging dashboard for community training. Project L.I.F.E. addresses this gap.

2.5 Theoretical Basis

The study is grounded in deliberate practice and motor-learning theory, which holds that skills are acquired through repetition with immediate, specific feedback (Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer, 1993). The single prioritized coaching cue and the end-of-session summary are designed around this principle. The study also draws on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which explains user adoption through perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use (Davis, 1989); this informs the one-click start, the guided readiness checks and the evaluation of usability.

Chapter 3Methodology

3.1 Research Design

The study uses a developmental research design with quantitative evaluation. The system is built through an iterative, prototype-based process (Agile), with automated tests run at every iteration, then tested for detection accuracy against a reference method and evaluated by users through a standardized questionnaire.

3.2 Development Methodology

  1. Requirements gathering. Review the 2025 AHA CPR guidelines, consult a trainer or certified first-aider, and identify functional, security and privacy requirements.
  2. Design. Plan the system architecture, vision algorithms, database schema and user interface, including the step-by-step CPR guide and the role-based access model.
  3. Development. Implement the in-browser vision pipeline, scoring and coaching logic, the Node.js/Express API and the React dashboard in short iterations, with version control (Git).
  4. Testing. Run automated unit and API tests, end-to-end browser tests, accuracy testing against a reference, and performance testing.
  5. Evaluation and refinement. Collect user feedback, calibrate thresholds through the Settings page, and refine the interface.

3.3 System Architecture

All components run on one laptop. The browser and the server communicate only over the computer's internal network interface, so the system works without internet access and is not reachable from other devices by default. Figure 2 shows the three layers.

1. Capture and analysis layer (browser, TypeScript). The webcam stream is read with the browser's media API. MediaPipe Pose Landmarker (WebAssembly, GPU with CPU fallback) extracts 33 body landmarks per frame, and the Hand Landmarker adds 21 landmarks per hand about eight times per second. A vision pipeline, running outside the user-interface framework for speed, locks the evaluated arm, computes the smoothed elbow angle, detects compressions from wrist motion, scores each compression and selects a coaching cue.

2. Feedback layer (React dashboard). The Live Session page shows the camera view with a skeleton overlay of the evaluated arm (green when locked, red when bent), elbow angle, current BPM, the trainee's fingers (gold when the hands are stacked), a large color-coded coaching cue, readiness checks, an optional Web Audio metronome and optional spoken cues, the session timer and the step-by-step CPR panel. A summary with a compression timeline and the procedure checklist appears at the end.

3. Data and dashboard layer (Node.js, Express, SQLite). Each completed session is first stored in the browser (IndexedDB) and then sent to the local REST API, which validates it and writes it in a single transaction to an SQLite database (better-sqlite3, write-ahead logging). Unsent sessions upload automatically when the server is available. The dashboard shows statistics, a live monitor that refreshes every 2.5 seconds, the leaderboard, session history, trainee progress, exports and settings.

Access is controlled by user accounts with three roles. Trainers manage trainees and run sessions; administrators additionally manage settings, staff accounts, test-data exports and session deletion. When an administrator adds a trainee, the system can create the trainee's own sign-in account: the username is generated from the trainee's name and a random temporary password is shown once to the administrator, who passes it to the trainee. Trainee accounts can view only their own progress (sessions, accuracy trend and procedure results) and the CPR guide, and can start their own practice sessions: the Live Session opens locked to the trainee's own profile, the server accepts sessions only for that trainee and marks them as self-practice, and the trainer's live monitor and history show them. Self-practice sessions are excluded from the leaderboard and from all dashboard statistics (sessions today, average accuracy and rate, the 30-day accuracy trend and the top-five ranking), which use trainer-supervised sessions only; they remain visible in the trainee's progress, the session history and the dashboard's recent-activity list. Every other staff function is closed to trainee accounts, and deactivating a trainee disables the account. Passwords are hashed with bcrypt, usernames are matched without regard to letter case, sign-in uses signed tokens that can be revoked, repeated failed sign-ins are locked out temporarily, and initial or temporary passwords must be changed at first sign-in.

3.5 Physical Prototype Setup (L.I.F.E. Training Station)

The DIY chest prop is placed on a stable surface or floor mat. The laptop is positioned 1.5 to 2.5 meters away at chest height so the webcam captures the trainee's shoulder, elbow and wrist from a side or 45-degree angle. Lighting is kept even, with no bright window behind the trainee, and the background is uncluttered to improve landmark detection. Before each session, the Live Session page confirms that the model is loaded, the camera is on, the trainee is in view, and the shoulder, elbow and wrist have been visible for one second; only then can the session start. The setup is documented with photographs for the poster and recorded for the video pitch.

3.6 Algorithm and Computation

3.6.1 Arm Selection and Elbow Angle (Locked Elbows)

Before the session starts, the system tracks the visibility of both arms and, at the start, locks onto the arm more visible to the camera. It keeps that arm for the whole session unless it disappears for more than one second while the other arm is visible, so the two arms are never mixed in one measurement. For each frame, the system takes the shoulder (A), elbow (B) and wrist (C) landmarks of the locked arm and computes the angle at the elbow from vectors BA and BC:

θ = arccos( (BA · BC) / (|BA| × |BC|) ), converted to degrees

Landmarks below the visibility threshold (default 0.5) are ignored, and a moving average over the last frames (default 5) smooths noise. The elbow is classified as locked when the smoothed angle is at or above a calibrated threshold (default 160 degrees). If the arm is not visible, the elbow is reported as not visible rather than locked, and the smoothing window is cleared after a gap longer than 300 milliseconds so that old angles are not reused.

3.6.2 Compression Cadence

The vertical position of the wrist landmark over time forms a roughly periodic signal during compressions. The system:

  1. Smooths the signal with a centered moving average over ±70 ms, which averages at least three frames at any frame rate from 15 to 30 FPS.
  2. Detects each compression as a local maximum of the wrist height whose prominence within a ±300 ms window exceeds a threshold (default 1.2% of the frame height) and that is at least 400 ms after the previous compression. Because the windows are defined in time rather than in frames, results do not depend on the camera frame rate.
  3. Refines each peak time between frames by fitting a parabola through the three samples around it, which removes the error caused by frame timing (up to about 3 BPM per reading at 30 FPS).
  4. Computes the cadence from the median of the latest N intervals (default 4):

BPM = 60,000 / median(latest N intervals in milliseconds)

Using the median of recent intervals makes the cadence reflect what the trainee is doing now and ignores a single missed or extra detection. A gap longer than 1.5 seconds (for example, rescue breaths) starts a new run, so the first compressions after a pause are not classified as too slow; the coach then prompts the trainee to resume and keep pauses short. Cadence is classified as in range between 100 and 120 BPM inclusive, and as too slow or too fast otherwise.

Calibration of the defaults. The detection defaults were calibrated on a physical model of a trainee seen by a typical 720p laptop webcam (vertical field of view about 42°): wrists and shoulders move by the compression depth, landmark positions carry random jitter of up to 0.6% of the frame, and the trainee may sway. Across camera distances of 1.5 to 3 m, compression depths of 4 to 6 cm, rates of 90 to 130 per minute and 15 to 30 FPS, a 2% threshold missed up to 61% of compressions at 2.5 m and over half at 15 FPS, while thresholds below 1% produced false compressions from jitter while the trainee was still. The chosen combination (1.2% threshold, ±70 ms smoothing) detected 94–100% of compressions at 20–30 FPS within 1.5–2.5 m (78–91% at 15 FPS), with a mean rate error of 0.8–1.7 compressions per minute and at most one false compression per 100 seconds while still. Elbow angles 10° or more from the 160° threshold were classified correctly at least 97% of the time, and 5° away at least 94%. These figures come from simulation; the accuracy study with participants (Section 3.8) provides the real-world values.

3.6.3 Accuracy Scoring and Coaching

Each detected compression is evaluated against two criteria: (a) the elbow was locked at the moment of the compression (read from the frame nearest the peak time) and (b) the local cadence was within 100 to 120 BPM. The first compression of each run is scored once the next compression gives its interval. A compression is unscorable if the elbow was not visible or if it was a lone compression with no cadence; unscorable compressions are reported separately and excluded from the denominator. The session accuracy is:

Accuracy (%) = (compressions meeting both criteria / scorable compressions) × 100

A session passes when accuracy is at or above the pass mark (default 80%, a placeholder to be confirmed with a qualified first-aid trainer). A session is not saved if the camera failed, if the trainee was out of view for most of it, or if no compression could be scored, so invalid sessions do not distort results. During the session, one coaching cue is shown at a time, chosen in priority order (step into view, make the arm visible, start or resume compressions, lock your elbows, push faster, slow down, good) from the last three scored compressions, and changed no more than once every 1.5 seconds to avoid flicker.

3.6.4 Hand Position and the Step-by-Step CPR Sequence

A second MediaPipe model, the Hand Landmarker, detects up to two hands with 21 landmarks each (the wrist and four points per finger) about eight times per second, so the compression timing from pose tracking keeps its full frame rate. The hands are classified as stacked when the centers of the two palms are within one palm length of each other. Because one hand often hides the other from a side view, the system falls back to the two wrist landmarks of the pose model, which must be within 40% of a forearm length. The hand reading nearest each compression is recorded, and the session reports the percentage of compressions with stacked hands. Hand position is reported separately and does not change the accuracy score.

During the session, a step-by-step panel follows the 2025 AHA adult sequence. Steps 1 and 2 (scene, response, breathing, calling 911 and an AED) are confirmed by the trainer strictly in order: each item unlocks only after the previous one, unticking an item clears the items after it, and compressions cannot start until all four are confirmed, unless the trainer chooses compression-only practice, in which case those steps are recorded as not done. Before starting, the trainer selects the victim (adult, child or infant) and the number of rescuers, which sets the compressions per cycle from Table 1 (30, or 15 for a child or infant with two rescuers). The panel counts compressions in the current cycle and, at the end of each cycle, prompts the trainee to give 2 breaths; it also shows progress toward the expected number of cycles in 2 minutes (about 5 cycles of 30:2, or about 10 of 15:2); when compressions resume after a pause of at least 1.5 seconds the cycle is counted as complete, and a pause longer than 10 seconds triggers a prompt to resume. When the AED arrives, the trainer walks through turning it on, attaching the pads, standing clear and the shock or no-shock outcome, after which the trainee is prompted to resume CPR immediately. If the trainee keeps compressing past the end of a cycle, the prompt escalates to "Stop — give 2 breaths now", and any interruption longer than 10 seconds (including the restart after the AED) is counted. The 2-minute session timer marks when to check the person and switch compressors. At the end of the session the system evaluates procedure adherence as a checklist: scene safety, responsiveness, breathing and the emergency call done before compressions; compressions started; hands stacked on at least 70% of compressions (when measured); 2 breaths after every cycle; about the expected number of cycles for the time elapsed; every pause under 10 seconds; and correct AED use when an AED was used. Items that do not apply are left out, and the procedure score is the percentage of applicable items followed. It is reported and stored separately; the Pass or Needs Improvement result remains based on compression accuracy. Because the camera scoring is designed for adult technique, child and infant selections change only the ratio and counts; the elbow and hand checks are not applicable to infant two-finger or two-thumb compressions.

3.8 Data Gathering Procedure and Participants

Testing is done in two parts:

  1. Accuracy testing. Participants (for example, 10 to 30 students or club members) perform compression sets on the prop under varying conditions (good and poor lighting, different distances and camera angles, and deliberately incorrect posture and rhythm, including bent elbows and rates of about 90 and 130 BPM). For each session, the system's per-compression output (time, rate, elbow angle and locked status of every compression) is exported as CSV from the Reports & Export page. The evaluation script turns the export into a label sheet with one row per detected compression. A trained observer, assisted by a video recording, labels each compression as locked or not locked, adds any compression the system missed, and records the test condition (for example, “dim light” or “3 m”); the reference rate is set with a metronome or a timed count. The script then compares the system with the observer's labels, overall and for each condition.
  2. Procedure and performance data. For every supervised session the system also records the trainer-confirmed CPR sequence, the cycles completed, skipped breaths, pauses over 10 seconds, the share of compressions with the hands stacked, the procedure score, and the average frame rate and pose-inference time. The sessions export lists these with one row per session, including each procedure item as followed, missed or not applicable. Self-practice sessions are marked and left out of the analysis.
  3. User evaluation. After using the system, participants and trainers answer the evaluation questionnaire (Section 3.9).

Sampling is purposive, selecting students and trainers with some interest or background in first aid. The exact sample size is to be confirmed with the adviser.

3.9 Evaluation Instrument

The system is evaluated using the ISO/IEC 25010 software product quality model, covering functional suitability, performance efficiency, usability, reliability, security and portability. A 5-point Likert scale is used (Table 5).

The draft questionnaire (provided with the project as Project_LIFE_Evaluation_Questionnaire.pdf) has 24 statements, four for each characteristic: functional suitability (FS1–FS4, e.g. “The system correctly shows whether my elbows are locked during compressions”), performance efficiency (PE1–PE4, e.g. “The feedback on the screen appears without noticeable delay”), usability (US1–US4), reliability (RE1–RE4), security (SE1–SE4, e.g. “Trainees can see only their own results”) and portability (PO1–PO4, e.g. “The system works without an internet connection once installed”). Each respondent also indicates whether they are a trainee or a trainer, so the two groups can be compared. The questionnaire is to be validated by experts (for example, an IT instructor and a first-aid trainer) before use.

3.10 Statistical Treatment

  • Weighted mean and standard deviation of the Likert responses for each item, each ISO/IEC 25010 characteristic and overall, interpreted with Table 5, for all respondents and separately for trainees and trainers.
  • Confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, specificity and F1-score for locked-elbow classification (positive class: locked), and the same measures for "correct compression" (locked and in range).
  • Compression detection rate: matched, missed and extra detections against the observer's labels, within a ±250 ms tolerance.
  • The accuracy, F1-score, BPM error and detection results for each test condition (lighting, distance, camera angle, intended error).
  • Mean absolute error (MAE), root-mean-square error and bias of the measured BPM against the reference BPM, and the share of readings within ±5 BPM.
  • Pearson correlation between the system and reference BPM, and a paired t-test of their difference (significance level 0.05).
  • Procedure adherence: mean and standard deviation of the percentage of applicable CPR procedure items followed per session, and the frequency of each missed item (for example, skipped breaths or pauses over 10 seconds) across sessions and participants.
  • Mean, standard deviation and extremes of the average frame rate and pose-inference time per session, recorded automatically, for performance efficiency.

All of these are computed with the project's evaluation script (scripts/evaluate.mjs): the accuracy measures from the test-data export and the observer's label sheet, procedure adherence and performance from the sessions export, and the questionnaire results from a spreadsheet of the responses (one row per respondent).

3.12 Ethical Considerations

  • Participants give informed consent (and parental or guardian consent for minors), and participation is voluntary. The system enforces this: a session, including a trainee's self-practice session, cannot be saved until consent is recorded.
  • Camera video is processed in the browser and is never uploaded or stored; only numerical metrics are saved. Any separate video recording for observer labelling is made only with the participant's agreement.
  • Trainee sign-in accounts are created only by an administrator; temporary passwords are shown once and must be replaced by the trainee, and deactivating a trainee immediately disables the account.
  • Data stays on the training computer, which by default does not accept connections from other devices. Trainee names and scores are shown only to signed-in trainers and administrators, each trainee account can see only that trainee's own results (not trainer notes or other trainees), the leaderboard can hide names, and exports are restricted by role, in line with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173).
  • The system is presented clearly as a practice aid and not a substitute for accredited CPR certification; certificates state that they confirm practice-session completion only.
  • All sources are properly cited, and the open-source licenses of MediaPipe and other libraries are respected.
