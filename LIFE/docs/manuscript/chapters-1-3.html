Chapter 1Introduction

1.1 Background of the Study

Sudden cardiac arrest remains one of the most common causes of death that could have been prevented. Whether a person survives often comes down to how soon someone nearby starts cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), since every minute without good chest compressions lowers the odds of recovery. The American Heart Association (AHA) updated its Guidelines for CPR and Emergency Cardiovascular Care in October 2025. For adults, a rescuer is told to check the scene and the person, call for help and an automated external defibrillator (AED), and then push hard and fast: at least 5 cm (2 inches) deep, 100 to 120 times a minute, letting the chest come back up fully and stopping as little as possible [Cite: AHA, 2025]. Good compressions are given with straight, locked elbows, so that the rescuer's body weight goes down through the arms and into the patient's chest.

Knowing the guidelines is only half of it. CPR is a physical skill, and like any physical skill it has to be practiced again and again, with someone or something pointing out mistakes, until the right posture and rhythm become automatic. Training manikins with built-in sensors do this job well, but they are costly. In the Philippines a single unit can cost tens of thousands of pesos, which puts it out of reach for many public schools, student safety clubs and barangay emergency responders. Many learners therefore get a lecture or a short demonstration and very little of the one-on-one, corrected practice that actually builds the skill.

Recent progress in computer vision opens a cheaper route. Lightweight pose-estimation models such as Google's MediaPipe Pose Landmarker can find 33 body landmarks in real time using an ordinary webcam [Cite: Bazarevsky et al., 2020; Lugaresi et al., 2019]. With the MediaPipe Tasks Vision library, the model runs right inside a web browser through WebAssembly and the computer's graphics processor. It needs no cloud connection and nothing installed apart from the browser. This means posture (the elbow angle) and rhythm (the compression rate) can be measured on the kind of laptop most schools already have.

Project L.I.F.E. (Localized Intelligent First-aid Evaluator) puts this idea into practice. It is an offline system: a laptop webcam and pose estimation running in the browser watch a trainee doing compressions on a cheap DIY chest prop. While the trainee works, the system shows coaching on screen (and can speak it aloud), tracks the hands and fingers, and walks the session through the 2025 AHA adult CPR sequence one step at a time. Results are saved in a local database behind a web dashboard built with React and TypeScript, served by a Node.js server that uses SQLite. Trainers can follow each trainee's progress, rank performance and export the data. Trainees can sign in to check their own progress and practice on their own. None of this needs internet access or expensive equipment, and the camera video never leaves the computer.

1.2 Statement of the Problem

This study responds to the shortage of affordable CPR training tools that can give feedback, especially in communities with limited resources. In particular, it tries to answer the following questions:

  1. What are the limitations of the CPR training methods and tools currently available to public schools and community responders in terms of cost, accessibility and feedback?
  2. How can a webcam-based pose-estimation system that runs in the browser be designed to detect elbow extension and compression cadence during CPR practice and coach the trainee in real time?
  3. How accurately does the system detect locked elbows and compression rate when compared with a reference method (a trained observer and a metronome-timed count)?
  4. How can session results, trainee records, procedure adherence and role-based access for administrators, trainers and trainees be stored, secured and displayed through a local, offline web dashboard?
  5. How do trainers and trainees rate the system in terms of functional suitability, performance efficiency, usability, reliability, security and portability?

1.3 Objectives of the Study

General Objective. To design, develop and evaluate Project L.I.F.E., an offline computer-vision CPR training evaluator that gives real-time feedback on compression posture and cadence using only a standard webcam and a web browser.

Specific Objectives.

  1. To develop a computer-vision module in TypeScript, using MediaPipe Tasks Vision (Pose Landmarker and Hand Landmarker), that runs in the browser, detects upper-body and hand landmarks, and computes the elbow angle, compression cadence and hand position in real time.
  2. To give real-time feedback on the live camera view: whether the elbows are locked, the current cadence (BPM), one coaching cue at a time chosen by priority (for example, "Push faster" or "Lock your elbows"), an optional metronome and optional spoken cues. Alongside this, the system guides the trainee step by step through the 2025 AHA adult CPR sequence, with breath prompts at the correct compression-to-breath ratio, a guided AED pause and the 2-minute compressor switch.
  3. To build a local web dashboard (React and TypeScript, served by Node.js and Express with an SQLite database) that works entirely offline. It stores trainee profiles and consent, sessions, per-compression data and the trainer-confirmed CPR sequence. It provides a live session monitor, a leaderboard, session history, PDF reports with CSV exports and a Certificate of Practice Completion. Administrators, trainers and trainees each get the access their role needs, and trainees can have their own sign-in accounts to review their progress and practice on their own.
  4. To design a low-cost DIY chest prop that can stand in for a commercial manikin during practice.
  5. To evaluate how accurately the system detects posture and rate (confusion matrix, precision, recall, F1-score and BPM error), how closely trainees follow the CPR procedure, and the overall quality of the system using the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model.

1.4 Significance of the Study

Trainees and students. They get immediate, objective feedback on their posture, their rhythm and the order of the CPR steps, so they can fix mistakes while practicing instead of waiting for an instructor to review them later. With their own sign-in, they can also look back at their progress and practice between supervised sessions.

Trainers and student safety clubs. They get a record of each trainee's progress over time, can rank performance, and have data to back up decisions about who is ready for formal certification.

Public schools. Because the system runs on laptops the school already owns, a free web browser and inexpensive materials, cost becomes much less of a barrier. This helps schools with tight budgets and unreliable internet offer first-aid training.

Barangay responders and the community. Responders who are better trained make the community more ready for emergencies and may improve the survival chances of people who suffer cardiac arrest.

Future researchers. The study leaves behind a documented and tested starting point, including open evaluation tools, for low-cost, vision-based skills training that others can extend to different first-aid procedures.

The study also supports the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, in particular SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and SDG 4 (Quality Education).

1.5 Scope and Delimitations

Scope.

  • Real-time detection of elbow extension (locked or not locked) and compression cadence (target 100 to 120 BPM) for one trainee at a time. A practice session lasts 2 minutes by default, in line with the AHA advice to switch compressors every 2 minutes.
  • A standard laptop webcam placed to the side of the trainee or at a 45-degree angle to the upper body.
  • Offline use in a web browser (Chrome or Edge) on one computer, with a local Node.js server and an SQLite database. The system starts from a single desktop shortcut.
  • A step-by-step guide through the 2025 AHA adult CPR sequence in every session. The trainer confirms the scene, responsiveness, breathing and emergency-call steps; the camera tracks the compressions; and the system counts cycles and prompts for breaths at the ratio for the chosen victim and number of rescuers (30:2, or 15:2 for a child or infant with two rescuers), or runs hands-only CPR. It also guides an AED pause and signals the 2-minute compressor switch.
  • Hand and finger tracking to check that the trainee's hands are stacked, one on top of the other.
  • Practice on a DIY chest prop (high-density foam, stacked cardboard, or a rolled towel inside a shirt) marked with a target X.
  • Optional sign-in accounts for trainees, created by an administrator, which let each trainee see their own progress and run self-practice sessions.
  • Records of trainee profiles and consent, session results and per-compression data, a leaderboard, PDF reports and CSV exports for analysis, and a Certificate of Practice Completion that can be downloaded as a PDF.

Delimitations.

  • The system is a training aid, not a medical device. It does not certify that anyone is competent to perform CPR in real life, and it is never used on real patients.
  • The system does not measure compression depth, chest recoil, exact hand placement on the center of the chest, or how effective the rescue breaths are, because a single 2D webcam cannot measure these reliably. The trainer remains responsible for watching them. The system only checks that the hands are stacked and prompts for breaths in 30:2 mode.
  • The elbow angle is measured in two dimensions and is most accurate from the side. Only the arm nearest the camera is evaluated.
  • The camera evaluates adult CPR technique only. Choosing a child or infant victim changes just the compression-to-breath ratio and the cycle count used for practice. Child and infant compression technique, including the two-finger or two-thumb method for infants, is not evaluated.
  • Accuracy can change with lighting, camera angle, clothing and anything blocking the camera's view. Testing is carried out indoors under controlled conditions.
  • Participants come only from the selected school or organization in Iligan City, so the results may not apply to every population.
  • The study evaluates the offline installation on the training laptop. An online copy of the dashboard can also be hosted for demonstrations. When that copy is set up to keep its data, trainee records are stored in a cloud database service (Turso) instead of on the training computer. The online copy is not used to collect the study's data.

1.6 Definition of Terms

  • Accuracy score. The percentage of scorable compressions in a session that meet both the posture and the cadence criteria.
  • AED (Automated External Defibrillator). A portable device that checks the heart rhythm and, when needed, delivers a shock while giving spoken instructions.
  • CPR cycle. One set of chest compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths. For an adult this is 30 compressions and 2 breaths (a 30:2 ratio); about 5 cycles of 30:2 take roughly 2 minutes.
  • Compression cadence. How many chest compressions are given per minute, expressed in beats per minute (BPM).
  • CPR (Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation). An emergency procedure of chest compressions, with rescue breaths given by trained rescuers, that keeps blood flowing when the heart stops.
  • DIY chest prop. A cheap, improvised chest simulator used in place of a commercial CPR manikin.
  • Hand Landmarker. A MediaPipe model that finds 21 landmarks on each detected hand (the wrist and four points on each finger). Here it is used to check that the hands are stacked.
  • Hands-only CPR. Continuous chest compressions without rescue breaths, recommended for rescuers who are untrained or unwilling to give breaths.
  • Leaderboard. A list of trainees ranked by average accuracy, with the option to hide names when it is shown in public.
  • Locked elbows. Arms held fully straight, so the rescuer's body weight passes through them. In this system, it means an elbow angle at or above a calibrated threshold (160 degrees by default).
  • MediaPipe Pose Landmarker. An open-source Google model, available through the MediaPipe Tasks Vision library, that finds 33 body landmarks in images or video in real time, each with coordinates and a visibility score.
  • Offline system. A system that runs entirely on local hardware and does not need an internet connection.
  • Procedure adherence score. The percentage of applicable CPR procedure items a trainee followed in a session (for example, doing the steps in order, giving breaths after every cycle, keeping pauses under 10 seconds). It is reported separately from the accuracy score.
  • Pose estimation. A computer-vision technique that finds the main body joints in images or video.
  • Scorable compression. A detected compression for which both the elbow angle and the cadence could be measured. Compressions where the elbow was out of view are reported separately as unscorable.
  • Self-practice session. A session a trainee starts on their own sign-in account. It is saved to the trainee's progress and to the session history but is left out of the leaderboard and the dashboard statistics.
  • SQLite. A self-contained relational database engine that stores everything in a single file and does not need a separate database server.
  • Supervised session. A session run by a trainer or administrator. Only supervised sessions count toward rankings and dashboard statistics.
  • WebAssembly. A portable binary format that lets compiled code, such as the pose model's runtime, run at close to native speed inside a web browser.

2.1 Related Literature

2.1.1 Cardiac Arrest and the Adult CPR Sequence

Few people survive a cardiac arrest outside the hospital, but quick CPR from a bystander can make a large difference. The 2025 AHA guidelines lay out a sequence for adult bystanders. First, make sure the scene is safe, tap the person and shout, and treat absent or gasping breathing as cardiac arrest. Second, call the emergency number (911 in the Philippines) and send someone for an AED. Third, start chest compressions with the heel of one hand on the center of the chest and the other hand on top, pushing at least 5 cm deep at 100 to 120 per minute, letting the chest recoil fully and keeping interruptions short. Fourth, if trained, give cycles of 30 compressions and 2 breaths; otherwise continue hands-only CPR, and avoid giving too many breaths. Fifth, use the AED as soon as it arrives and go straight back to CPR after a shock or a no-shock message. Sixth, keep going, switching compressors every 2 minutes, until responders take over or the person recovers [Cite: AHA, 2025].

One cycle is 30 compressions and 2 breaths. The ratio depends on the victim and on how many rescuers are present (Table 1): 30:2 for adults with one or two rescuers and for children and infants with one rescuer, and 15:2 for children and infants when there are two rescuers. At 100 to 120 compressions per minute, about 5 cycles of 30:2 take roughly 2 minutes. The rescuers then check the person and switch compressors, and they repeat this until help arrives or the person shows signs of life. The guidelines are clear that the quality of the compressions (rate, depth, recoil and few interruptions) has a major effect on survival [Cite: Philippine Heart Association or Philippine Red Cross basic life support materials].

2.1.2 CPR Training and Feedback

Research on resuscitation training generally finds that learners who practice hands-on with real-time feedback pick up the skill faster, and keep it longer, than those who only attend a lecture or watch a video. Audio prompts such as a metronome also help learners hold the right rate. The catch is that commercial feedback manikins and devices are expensive [Cite: studies on CPR feedback devices, metronome guidance and deliberate practice].

2.1.3 Computer Vision and Human Pose Estimation

Human pose estimation finds the joints of the body in images or video, and today's lightweight models can do it in real time on ordinary hardware. MediaPipe Pose, built on the BlazePose architecture, returns 33 landmarks, each with normalized coordinates and a visibility score. That makes it well suited to measuring joint angles on an everyday laptop (Bazarevsky et al., 2020). The MediaPipe framework arranges perception models into efficient pipelines (Lugaresi et al., 2019). Its Tasks Vision library runs the Pose Landmarker in the browser through WebAssembly, with optional GPU acceleration, so there is no need for a Python installation or a model running on a server.

2.1.4 Pose Estimation in Exercise and Skill Coaching

Pose-estimation systems are already used to correct exercise form, support physical therapy and coach athletes. They usually compute joint angles from sets of three landmarks and count repetitions from the regular movement of a single landmark. CPR fits the same pattern, because compressions are a repeated up-and-down motion of the upper body and wrists [Cite: fitness posture-correction and rehabilitation studies].

2.1.5 Offline and Low-Resource Educational Technology

Many schools in developing regions have weak or unstable internet. A locally hosted web application, where a small server and a file-based database run on the same computer as the browser, lets a school use a tool without any cloud infrastructure or separate database server. This keeps costs down, reduces setup to a single desktop shortcut, and avoids the privacy risk of uploading video of trainees [Cite: studies on offline learning tools in low-resource settings].

2.1.6 Frugal Innovation

Frugal innovation is about building something that works for a fraction of the usual cost by making use of what is already available. Swapping a specialized manikin for a DIY prop and free software on a laptop the school already owns is a clear example of this approach [Cite: frugal innovation literature].

2.3 Synthesis and Research Gap

Taken together, the literature points to four things. CPR quality depends on the right rate and posture within a defined rescue sequence. Practice with feedback improves learning. Pose estimation can measure posture and movement from ordinary cameras, now even inside a web browser. And cost and connectivity still keep training tools out of reach in many communities. Current solutions depend either on expensive sensor hardware or on special cameras, technical installations and cloud services. Very little work has been done on an evaluator that runs fully offline in a browser with only a webcam, and that combines posture and cadence feedback, guidance through the whole CPR sequence, and a secure local dashboard for keeping records in community training. Project L.I.F.E. is meant to fill that gap.

2.5 Theoretical Basis

The study rests on deliberate practice and motor-learning theory, which hold that people learn a skill by repeating it and getting immediate, specific feedback each time (Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer, 1993). The single coaching cue, chosen by priority, and the summary at the end of each session were designed with this in mind. The study also draws on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which explains whether people adopt a technology in terms of how useful and how easy to use they find it (Davis, 1989). TAM shaped the one-click start and the guided readiness checks, and it informs how usability is evaluated.

Chapter 3Methodology

3.1 Research Design

The study uses a developmental research design with a quantitative evaluation. The researchers build the system in short, prototype-based iterations (Agile) and run the automated tests at every iteration. The finished system is then tested for detection accuracy against a reference method and rated by its users through a standardized questionnaire.

3.2 Development Methodology

  1. Requirements gathering. Study the 2025 AHA CPR guidelines, consult a trainer or certified first-aider, and list the functional, security and privacy requirements.
  2. Design. Plan the system architecture, the vision algorithms, the database schema and the user interface, including the step-by-step CPR guide and the role-based access model.
  3. Development. Build the in-browser vision pipeline, the scoring and coaching logic, the Node.js/Express API and the React dashboard in short iterations, with version control (Git).
  4. Testing. Run automated unit and API tests, end-to-end browser tests, accuracy tests against a reference, and performance tests.
  5. Evaluation and refinement. Gather feedback from users, calibrate the thresholds through the Settings page, and improve the interface.

3.3 System Architecture

Everything runs on one laptop. The browser and the server talk to each other only through the computer's internal network interface, so the system works without internet access and, by default, cannot be reached from other devices. Figure 2 shows the three layers.

1. Capture and analysis layer (browser, TypeScript). The browser's media API reads the webcam stream. MediaPipe Pose Landmarker (WebAssembly, using the GPU with a CPU fallback) picks out 33 body landmarks in every frame, and the Hand Landmarker adds 21 landmarks per hand about eight times a second. A vision pipeline, kept outside the user-interface framework so it runs faster, locks onto the arm being evaluated, computes the smoothed elbow angle, detects compressions from the wrist's movement, scores each compression and chooses a coaching cue.
▼
2. Feedback layer (React dashboard). The Live Session page shows the camera view with a skeleton drawn over the evaluated arm (green when it is locked, red when it is bent), the elbow angle, the current BPM, and the trainee's fingers (gold when the hands are stacked). It also shows a large coaching cue in color, the readiness checks, an optional Web Audio metronome and optional spoken cues, the session timer and the step-by-step CPR panel. When the session ends, a summary appears with a timeline of the compressions and the procedure checklist.
▼
3. Data and dashboard layer (Node.js, Express, SQLite). Each finished session is saved in the browser first (IndexedDB) and then sent to the local REST API, which checks it and writes it to an SQLite database in a single transaction (better-sqlite3, write-ahead logging). Any session that could not be sent is uploaded automatically once the server is available again. The dashboard shows statistics, a live monitor that refreshes every 2.5 seconds, the leaderboard, session history, trainee progress, exports and settings.

Access is controlled through user accounts with three roles. Trainers manage trainees and run sessions. Administrators can do the same and also manage settings, staff accounts, test-data exports and the deletion of sessions. When an administrator adds a trainee, the system can create a sign-in account for that trainee. The username is generated from the trainee's name, and a random temporary password is shown once to the administrator, who hands it to the trainee.

A trainee account can see only that trainee's own progress (sessions, accuracy trend and procedure results) and the CPR guide, and it can start practice sessions. In that case the Live Session opens locked to the trainee's own profile, the server accepts sessions only for that trainee and marks them as self-practice, and the sessions still appear on the trainer's live monitor and in the history. Self-practice sessions are kept out of the leaderboard and out of every dashboard statistic (sessions today, average accuracy and rate, the 30-day accuracy trend and the top-five ranking), all of which use trainer-supervised sessions only. They still show up in the trainee's progress, the session history and the dashboard's list of recent activity. All other staff functions are closed to trainee accounts, and deactivating a trainee disables the account.

Passwords are hashed with bcrypt, and usernames are matched without regard to letter case. Sign-in uses signed tokens that can be revoked, repeated failed sign-ins lead to a temporary lockout, and any initial or temporary password must be changed at first sign-in.

3.5 Physical Prototype Setup (L.I.F.E. Training Station)

The DIY chest prop sits on a stable surface or a floor mat. The laptop is placed 1.5 to 2.5 meters away at chest height, so the webcam sees the trainee's shoulder, elbow and wrist from the side or from a 45-degree angle. The lighting should be even, with no bright window behind the trainee, and a plain background helps the model find the landmarks. Before each session, the Live Session page checks that the model has loaded, the camera is on, the trainee is in view, and the shoulder, elbow and wrist have been visible for one second. Only then can the session start. The setup is photographed for the poster and recorded for the video pitch.

3.6 Algorithm and Computation

3.6.1 Arm Selection and Elbow Angle (Locked Elbows)

Before the session begins, the system watches how visible each arm is. When the session starts, it locks onto whichever arm the camera sees better and stays with that arm for the whole session. It switches only if that arm disappears for more than one second while the other arm is visible, so the two arms are never mixed in a single measurement. For each frame, the system takes the shoulder (A), elbow (B) and wrist (C) landmarks of the locked arm and computes the angle at the elbow from the vectors BA and BC:

θ = arccos( (BA · BC) / (|BA| × |BC|) ), converted to degrees

Landmarks below the visibility threshold (0.5 by default) are ignored, and a moving average over the most recent frames (5 by default) smooths out noise. The elbow counts as locked when the smoothed angle is at or above a calibrated threshold (160 degrees by default). If the arm cannot be seen, the elbow is reported as not visible rather than as locked. After a gap longer than 300 milliseconds the smoothing window is cleared, so old angles are not carried over.

3.6.2 Compression Cadence

During compressions, the up-and-down position of the wrist landmark traces a roughly regular wave over time. The system processes it in four steps:

  1. It smooths the signal with a centered moving average over ±70 ms, which always covers at least three frames at any frame rate from 15 to 30 FPS.
  2. It counts a compression at each local maximum of the wrist height that stands out by more than a threshold (1.2% of the frame height by default) within a ±300 ms window and comes at least 400 ms after the previous compression. Because these windows are set in time rather than in frames, the results do not depend on the camera's frame rate.
  3. It pins down the exact time of each peak between frames by fitting a parabola through the three samples around it. This removes the error caused by frame timing, which can reach about 3 BPM per reading at 30 FPS.
  4. It computes the cadence from the median of the latest N intervals (4 by default):

BPM = 60,000 / median(latest N intervals in milliseconds)

Taking the median of the most recent intervals keeps the cadence focused on what the trainee is doing right now, and a single missed or extra detection does not throw it off. A gap longer than 1.5 seconds (for example, while giving rescue breaths) starts a new run, so the first compressions after a pause are not marked as too slow; the coach then reminds the trainee to resume and to keep pauses short. A cadence from 100 to 120 BPM, inclusive, is in range; anything outside that is too slow or too fast.

Calibration of the defaults. The detection defaults were calibrated on a physical model of a trainee as seen by a typical 720p laptop webcam (vertical field of view of about 42°). In this model the wrists and shoulders move by the compression depth, every landmark position carries random jitter of up to 0.6% of the frame, and the trainee may sway. The model covered camera distances of 1.5 to 3 m, compression depths of 4 to 6 cm, rates of 90 to 130 per minute and frame rates of 15 to 30 FPS. A 2% threshold missed up to 61% of compressions at 2.5 m and more than half at 15 FPS, while thresholds below 1% picked up false compressions from jitter when the trainee was not moving. The combination finally chosen (a 1.2% threshold with ±70 ms smoothing) detected 94–100% of compressions at 20–30 FPS within 1.5–2.5 m (78–91% at 15 FPS). Its mean rate error was 0.8–1.7 compressions per minute, and it produced at most one false compression per 100 seconds while the trainee was still. Elbow angles 10° or more away from the 160° threshold were classified correctly at least 97% of the time, and angles 5° away at least 94% of the time. These figures come from simulation. The accuracy study with real participants (Section 3.8) will provide the real-world values.

3.6.3 Accuracy Scoring and Coaching

Every detected compression is judged on two criteria: (a) the elbow was locked at the moment of the compression, read from the frame closest to the peak time, and (b) the local cadence was within 100 to 120 BPM. The first compression of each run is scored once the next compression supplies its interval. A compression is unscorable if the elbow could not be seen, or if it was a lone compression with no cadence. Unscorable compressions are reported on their own and left out of the denominator. The session accuracy is:

Accuracy (%) = (compressions meeting both criteria / scorable compressions) × 100

A session passes when its accuracy reaches the pass mark (80% by default, a placeholder still to be confirmed with a qualified first-aid trainer). A session is not saved if the camera failed, if the trainee was out of view for most of it, or if not a single compression could be scored, so invalid sessions do not skew the results. During the session only one coaching cue is shown at a time. It is picked in order of priority (step into view, make the arm visible, start or resume compressions, lock your elbows, push faster, slow down, good) based on the last three scored compressions, and it changes no more than once every 1.5 seconds so the screen does not flicker.

3.6.4 Hand Position and the Step-by-Step CPR Sequence

A second MediaPipe model, the Hand Landmarker, finds up to two hands with 21 landmarks each (the wrist and four points on each finger). It runs about eight times a second, which leaves the pose tracking free to time compressions at the full frame rate. The hands count as stacked when the centers of the two palms are within one palm length of each other. From the side, one hand often hides the other, so in that case the system falls back on the two wrist landmarks from the pose model, which must be within 40% of a forearm length of each other. The system records the hand reading closest to each compression and reports the percentage of compressions made with stacked hands. Hand position is reported separately and does not affect the accuracy score.

Throughout the session, a step-by-step panel follows the 2025 AHA adult sequence. The trainer confirms steps 1 and 2 (scene, response, breathing, calling 911 and an AED) strictly in order. Each item unlocks only after the one before it, unticking an item clears everything after it, and compressions cannot start until all four items are confirmed. The exception is compression-only practice, chosen by the trainer, in which case those steps are recorded as not done. Before starting, the trainer also picks the victim (adult, child or infant) and the number of rescuers, which sets the compressions per cycle from Table 1 (30, or 15 for a child or infant with two rescuers).

The panel counts the compressions in the current cycle and, at the end of each cycle, tells the trainee to give 2 breaths. It also shows progress toward the number of cycles expected in 2 minutes (about 5 cycles of 30:2, or about 10 of 15:2). When compressions resume after a pause of at least 1.5 seconds, the cycle is counted as complete, and a pause longer than 10 seconds brings up a prompt to resume. When the AED arrives, the trainer goes through turning it on, attaching the pads, standing clear, and the shock or no-shock result, after which the trainee is told to resume CPR immediately. If the trainee keeps compressing past the end of a cycle, the prompt becomes more urgent ("Stop — give 2 breaths now"), and every interruption longer than 10 seconds is counted, including the restart after the AED. The 2-minute session timer marks the moment to check the person and switch compressors.

When the session ends, the system checks procedure adherence against a checklist: scene safety, responsiveness, breathing and the emergency call done before compressions; compressions started; hands stacked on at least 70% of compressions (when this was measured); 2 breaths after every cycle; about the expected number of cycles for the time that passed; every pause under 10 seconds; and correct AED use when an AED was used. Items that do not apply are skipped, and the procedure score is the percentage of applicable items the trainee followed. This score is reported and stored separately; the Pass or Needs Improvement result is still based on compression accuracy. Because camera scoring is built for adult technique, choosing a child or infant only changes the ratio and the counts, and the elbow and hand checks do not apply to two-finger or two-thumb compressions on an infant.

3.8 Data Gathering Procedure and Participants

Data is gathered in three parts:

  1. Accuracy testing. Participants (for example, 10 to 30 students or club members) do sets of compressions on the prop under different conditions: good and poor lighting, different distances and camera angles, and posture and rhythm that are wrong on purpose, such as bent elbows and rates of about 90 and 130 BPM. For each session, the system's output for every compression (time, rate, elbow angle and locked status) is exported as CSV from the Reports & Export page. The evaluation script turns this export into a label sheet with one row per detected compression. A trained observer, helped by a video recording, marks each compression as locked or not locked, adds any compression the system missed, and notes the test condition (for example, “dim light” or “3 m”). The reference rate comes from a metronome or a timed count. The script then compares the system with the observer's labels, both overall and for each condition.
  2. Procedure and performance data. For every supervised session, the system also records the trainer-confirmed CPR sequence, the cycles completed, skipped breaths, pauses over 10 seconds, the share of compressions with the hands stacked, the procedure score, and the average frame rate and pose-inference time. The sessions export lists all of this with one row per session, including each procedure item marked as followed, missed or not applicable. Self-practice sessions are flagged and left out of the analysis.
  3. User evaluation. After using the system, participants and trainers answer the evaluation questionnaire (Section 3.9).

Participants are chosen through purposive sampling: students and trainers who have some interest or background in first aid. The exact sample size is still to be confirmed with the adviser.

The system is evaluated with the ISO/IEC 25010 software product quality model, covering functional suitability, performance efficiency, usability, reliability, security and portability. Responses use a 5-point Likert scale (Table 5).

3.10 Statistical Treatment

  • Weighted mean and standard deviation of the Likert responses for each item, for each ISO/IEC 25010 characteristic and overall, interpreted using Table 5, for all respondents and separately for trainees and trainers.
  • Confusion matrix, accuracy, precision, recall, specificity and F1-score for classifying locked elbows (positive class: locked), and the same measures for a "correct compression" (locked and in range).
  • Compression detection rate: matched, missed and extra detections compared with the observer's labels, within a ±250 ms tolerance.
  • Accuracy, F1-score, BPM error and detection results for each test condition (lighting, distance, camera angle, intended error).
  • Mean absolute error (MAE), root-mean-square error and bias of the measured BPM against the reference BPM, and the share of readings within ±5 BPM.
  • Pearson correlation between the system's BPM and the reference BPM, and a paired t-test of the difference between them (significance level 0.05).
  • Procedure adherence: the mean and standard deviation of the percentage of applicable CPR procedure items followed per session, and how often each item was missed (for example, skipped breaths or pauses over 10 seconds) across sessions and participants.
  • Mean, standard deviation and extremes of the average frame rate and pose-inference time per session, recorded automatically, as measures of performance efficiency.

The project's evaluation script (scripts/evaluate.mjs) computes all of these. It takes the accuracy measures from the test-data export and the observer's label sheet, procedure adherence and performance from the sessions export, and the questionnaire results from a spreadsheet of responses with one row per respondent.

3.12 Ethical Considerations

  • Participants give informed consent (with parental or guardian consent for minors), and taking part is voluntary. The system enforces this: no session, including a trainee's self-practice session, can be saved until consent has been recorded.
  • Camera video is processed in the browser and is never uploaded or stored; only numerical measurements are saved. Any separate video recording made for the observer's labelling is done only with the participant's agreement.
  • Only an administrator can create a trainee sign-in account. Temporary passwords are shown once and must be replaced by the trainee, and deactivating a trainee disables the account immediately.
  • The data stays on the training computer, which by default does not accept connections from other devices. Trainee names and scores are shown only to signed-in trainers and administrators. Each trainee account sees only that trainee's own results (not trainer notes or other trainees), the leaderboard can hide names, and exports are restricted by role, in line with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173).
  • If the optional online copy described in Section 1.5 is used, its records are kept by a cloud database service rather than on the training computer. It is therefore used only with test data or with the trainees' explicit consent, its access key is kept out of the source code, and it is never used for the study's data.
  • The system is presented plainly as a practice aid and not a replacement for accredited CPR certification. Certificates state that they confirm completion of a practice session only.
  • All sources are properly cited, and the open-source licenses of MediaPipe and the other libraries are respected.
